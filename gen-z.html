We Shaped the World That Shaped Them: Understanding Gen Z Through Our Collective Journey

October 15, 2025 Essay 15–20 minute read Co-authored with Kacie Quigley

Authors: Ken Quigley, a late boomer/early Gen X'er just striving to understand  ·  Kacie Quigley, Early Gen Z'er Trying to Teach Old Dogs New Tricks

In Nepal, Gen Z didn't just protest — they rebuilt. Using Discord and digital mobilization, they overthrew a failing political system and elected a new government without relying on inherited structures. This isn't nihilism as Oxford defines it (rejection of all meaning), but Nietzsche's nihilism in action: the collapse of inherited meaning systems forcing the creation of new meaning. As Nietzsche wrote, "The nihilist must become the creator of new values." Gen Z doesn't reject meaning — they reject inherited meaning, then build their own. This is the circumstance we have created, and it is not pathology. It is philosophy.

This paper proposes Nietzschean nihilism as the framework for understanding Gen Z's worldview — not as pathological disengagement, but as the necessary response to the collapse of inherited meaning systems. Born between 1997–2012, Gen Z rejects the promises of their predecessors (e.g., "hard work guarantees success") not because they lack meaning, but because those promises failed. This isn't nihilism (Oxford's rejection of all meaning); it's Nietzsche's nihilism: the crisis preceding meaning-making. We trace how Baby Boomers' decisions — from ending the gold standard to championing women's rights — created economic and institutional instability that shattered inherited meaning. Gen Z's "nihilism" is not despair; it's the revolt Nietzsche described: dismantling broken systems to build new ones. This framework reframes Gen Z's skepticism as philosophical maturity, not dysfunction. Labeling it "nihilism" is a failure of perspective — not a description of Gen Z.

The Uncomfortable Mirror

We shaped the world that shaped them.

That's the uncomfortable truth I've had to confront after years of wondering why Gen Z seems so different, so disconnected, soà nihilistic. For years, I held the bootstrap mentality that served my generation well. Work hard, follow the rules, respect authority, and success will follow. When I watched twenty-somethings question everything, seem paralyzed by choices, or appear indifferent to traditional motivators, my first instinct was simple: they needed to put on their big boy and girl pants and join society.

I was missing something profound.

If you feel defensive reading this, you're not alone. I felt the same way when I first started connecting these philosophical and historical dots. The resistance is naturalùacknowledging our role in creating current conditions feels like accepting blame. But this isn't about blame. This is about understanding. And understanding Gen Z requires understanding ourselves, our choices, and the world we collectively built.

What we call ôGen Z nihilismö isn't pathology. It's not laziness, entitlement, or moral failing. It's philosophical sophisticationùa worldview developed by a generation that inherited the cumulative effects of every major decision we've made since 1971. They didn't choose to be nihilists. We created a world that required them to become philosophers.

The Chain of Unintended Consequences

The 1971 Pivot Point We All Lived Through

On August 15, 1971, President Nixon made a decision that seemed purely technical: end the dollar's convertibility to gold (U.S. Department of State, Office of the Historian, n.d.). Most of us barely noticed. It was monetary policy, economics, something for experts to worry about. But removing that anchor was like removing a damùthe economic water had to go somewhere, and it went everywhere.

Within a decade, inflation exploded. The stable economic foundation that allowed single-income households to thrive cracked apart (Federal Reserve History, n.d.). Suddenly, what had been comfortable became impossible. The math was brutal and simple: two incomes weren't a lifestyle choice for most familiesùthey became an economic necessity (Discovery Alert, 2025).

I remember when my neighbor's wife ôhad toö get a job. That phrase carried weight then. It wasn't liberation or fulfillment, though those came later for many. It was survival in an economy that no longer supported the family structure we'd known.

The Social Movements We Championed

At the same time, we were championing necessary and overdue social changes. The Equal Rights Amendment, women's liberation, civil rightsùthese weren't abstract political movements. They were moral imperatives that reshaped how we thought about work, family, and opportunity (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2021). Women entered the workforce not just because they needed the money, but because barriers that had existed for generations finally began falling.

The legislative transformation was systematic and profound. The Equal Pay Act of 1963 mandated equal pay for equal work. Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 banned sex discrimination in employment. Title IX of 1972 opened educational opportunities that had been closed to women for centuries. The Equal Credit Opportunity Act of 1974 granted women the right to obtain credit cards and loans without a husband's consentùbefore this, women literally couldn't build independent financial lives. The Pregnancy Discrimination Act of 1978 made it illegal to discriminate against pregnant workers.

These programs were morally necessary and remarkably successful. Women's labor force participation jumped from 33.9% in 1950 to 57.5% by 1990ùa 70% increase. When Title IX passed in 1972, only 42% of college students were female; by 2001, women represented the majority of college graduates. These weren't abstract policy victoriesùthey were transformations that touched every family in America.

But success in removing barriers created new economic realities. Each generation experienced different phases of this transformation, and each phase made the ladder to middle-class stability progressively taller:

Boomers experienced the initial implementation when these programs first opened doors, often entering workforces where they were pioneers with limited competition for newly accessible positions. The economic foundation was still stable enough that even newly available opportunities provided clear paths to advancement.

Gen X lived through the full integration period, when women's workforce participation became normalized but the economic stability that had supported single-income households was already eroding from the 1971 monetary policy shift. They needed these programs' protections in an increasingly competitive job market.

Millennials entered a workforce where these protections were established rights, but where the sheer volume of qualified workersùincluding the full participation of women that these programs enabledùmeant increased competition for the same opportunities that previous generations had accessed more easily.

Gen Z inherited a world where these programs provide essential protections, but where the economic conditions beneath them have shifted so dramatically that achieving the same life outcomes their grandparents reached now requires navigating significantly higher barriers. The programs still exist and function, but the ladder they provide access to has grown much taller.

These movements converged with economic necessity in ways we couldn't have predicted. The result? By the 1980s, dual-income households became the norm, not the exception (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2007), supported by legal frameworks that made this transition possible. We didn't choose this transformationùhistory chose it for us. But our children lived with the consequences of both our moral victories and the economic pressures they unleashed.

The Latchkey Reality We Created

Think about your childhood experience compared to what we created for our children:

We Boomers came home to cookies and milk, to mothers who had spent their day focused on homemaking, to fathers who worked but were home by dinner. Our world had clear boundaries, predictable rhythms, constant adult supervision.

Gen X came home to notes on the refrigerator, to houses where both parents worked, to a new kind of independence born from necessity. They became the original latchkey kidsùnot by choice, but because the economy demanded it.

Millennials experienced a mixed reality. Some had the traditional supervision we remembered, others lived the latchkey experience, but most grew up with parents who still believed the old economic rules applied. Work hard, get a degree, follow the system.

Gen Z came home to empty houses as the baseline norm. By the time they were born, dual-income households weren't an adjustmentùthey were simply how families worked. These children didn't experience the transition we lived through. They were born into structural autonomy.

We didn't choose this progression. Each generation made the best decisions they could with the options available. But we created a world where children learned self-reliance not as a life skill, but as a survival mechanism.

How Philosophy Explains What We're Seeing

Understanding Gen Z requires understanding what happens when historical forces create specific psychological conditions. Three philosophersùNietzsche, Sartre, and Camusùprovide a framework for understanding not just what Gen Z thinks, but why they think it.

Nietzsche: When the Old Rules Stop Working

Friedrich Nietzsche wrote about nihilism as what happens when traditional values and institutions lose their power to provide meaning (Nietzsche, 1968). Think about watching your parents' marriage fall apart as a childùsuddenly, the idea of ôlove conquers allö becomes questionable. That questioning isn't cynicism; it's a reasonable response to witnessing foundational beliefs fail.

We lived through massive institutional collapses: Watergate taught us presidents lie, corporate scandals taught us companies don't care about employees, economic crashes taught us the system isn't as stable as promised (Pew Research Center, 2022). We developed healthy skepticism as adults who had once believed these institutions were solid.

But our children didn't develop skepticismùthey inherited distrust. They were born into a world where questioning authority wasn't a hard-learned lesson, it was simply how intelligent people navigated reality. We had to learn to doubt institutions; they never had the luxury of trusting them in the first place.

This isn't pathological distrustùit's inherited wisdom. While we were learning to question, they mastered the art of authentic questioning. Where we still sometimes long for the certainty we once had, they've learned to find meaning without needing institutional validation.

Sartre: The Weight of Total Freedom

Jean-Paul Sartre wrote about the burden of radical freedomùthe overwhelming responsibility of creating meaning and identity without predetermined guidelines (Sartre, 1966). Imagine being handed car keys at sixteen with no driving lessons, no rules of the road, no destination. That's the existential situation Sartre described, and it's exactly what we created for Gen Z.

The latchkey experience we established wasn't just about empty houses after school. It was about children making fundamental decisions about their time, safety, priorities, and values without adult guidance. While we were at workùout of economic necessityùthey were learning to navigate choices that previous generations made with extensive family and community input (Coontz, 2016).

This created what Sartre called ôradical freedomöùthe burden and gift of complete self-determination. Previous generations had their paths largely determined by social expectations, economic roles, and family traditions. Gen Z had to construct their identities from scratch, often with minimal adult input.

The result? They became comfortable with a level of self-definition that can look like indecision or lack of commitment to older generations. But it's actually sophisticated identity development. While we're still learning what authentic freedom meansùmany of us having followed prescribed paths for decadesùthey've been practicing it since childhood.

Camus: When Reality Doesn't Match the Promise

Albert Camus wrote about ôthe absurdöùthe fundamental disconnect between human expectations and reality (Camus, 1991). Picture being told your entire childhood that ôwork hard and you'll succeed,ö then entering a job market where that formula no longer applies. The absurd isn't the failure of hard work; it's the persistence of the promise in a world where it no longer holds.

We promised Gen Z a world that no longer existed. We told them that college would guarantee good jobs, that following the rules would ensure success, that the American Dream was available to anyone willing to work for it (Pew Research Center, 2019). These weren't liesùthey reflected our experience. But by the time Gen Z came of age, the economic and social foundations of these promises had shifted dramatically.

The gig economy, student debt crisis, housing affordability, and climate change created a fundamental gap between what we told them to expect and what they actually encountered. This isn't their failure to adaptùit's the natural response to discovering that the world operates differently than advertised.

Camus argued that once you recognize the absurd, you have three choices: suicide (literal or metaphorical giving up), hope for salvation (believing someone else will fix the disconnect), or revolt (engaging fully with life despite its contradictions). Gen Z chose revoltùnot in the sense of violence, but in the sense of refusing to pretend the contradictions don't exist.

While we sometimes struggle with uncertaintyùhaving been raised to expect linear progress and clear outcomesùthey've learned to engage with uncertainty as a permanent condition. They've mastered what Camus called ôlucid engagementöùparticipating fully in life while acknowledging its fundamental contradictions.

The Generational Ripple Effect

Each Generation's Formative Crucible

Every generation faces a unique set of historical circumstances that shapes their fundamental worldview. Understanding these formative experiences helps explain not just what each generation believes, but why their responses make perfect sense.

Boomers grew up in post-war optimism with clear hierarchies and functioning institutions. The economy was expanding, social roles were defined, and following established paths generally led to predicted outcomes. This created a generation comfortable with institutional trust and hierarchical thinkingùnot because they were naive, but because institutions and hierarchies actually worked during their formative years.

Gen X faced economic uncertainty, normalized divorce, and early independence. They developed resourcefulness and skepticismùnot as character flaws, but as necessary survival skills in a world less predictable than their parents had known. They learned to be self-reliant because the safety nets their parents relied on were becoming unreliable.

Millennials lived through the digital revolution with economic promises that were ultimately broken. They were told they were special, that technology would create unlimited opportunities, that globalization would benefit everyone. When these promises failedùthrough economic crashes, job market changes, and technological disruptionùthey developed a complex mix of technological optimism and economic pessimism.

Gen Z inherited all of these historical experiences plus climate crisis, social fragmentation, and economic instability as baseline conditions. They didn't experience the promise being brokenùthey were born after it was already broken. Their nihilism isn't disillusionment; it's clear-eyed assessment of inherited conditions.

Connecting the Dots

The pattern becomes clear when we stop judging each generation's response and start understanding their circumstances. Each generation's ôproblemsö were actually adaptations to the world they inherited from the previous generation.

Boomer institutional trust made sense in an era of functioning institutions. Gen X skepticism made sense when those institutions began failing. Millennial technological optimism made sense during the early internet boom. Gen Z philosophical nihilism makes sense in a world of permanent uncertainty and inherited distrust.

We all did our best with the information and options we had. The dual-income households, the latchkey arrangements, the institutional questioningùthese weren't failures of parenting or character. They were reasonable responses to historical circumstances.

But each adaptation created new conditions for the next generation. Our solutions became their starting points. Our hard-won wisdom became their inherited assumptions.

Burning Down the Pathology Myth

Let's address the elephant in the room directly: Gen Z nihilism isn't depression, laziness, or moral failing. It's philosophical maturity developed in response to world conditions that require philosophical thinking.

Every era produces what older generations call ônihilisticö youth. The Lost Generation of the 1920s, the Beatnik Generation of the 1950s, the counterculture of the 1960sùeach was labeled as disconnected, irresponsible, or morally deficient by their elders (Strauss & Howe, 1991). History suggests these ônihilisticö generations often develop exactly the perspectives their times require.

The real issue isn't Gen Z's nihilismùit's our tendency to judge their adaptation by our era's standards. We developed our values in a world of greater institutional stability, clearer career paths, and more predictable life trajectories. They developed their values in a world of constant change, institutional failure, and permanent uncertainty.

Their nihilismùtheir refusal to accept traditional sources of meaning without questionùmight be exactly what our uncertain world needs. While we sometimes struggle with ambiguity, they've learned to find meaning within it. While we sometimes need certainty to function effectively, they've learned to function effectively without certainty.

This isn't pathology. This is adaptation. And it might be the most sophisticated philosophical adaptation any generation has achieved.

What We Can Learn From Each Other

What Gen Z Can Teach Us

Authentic freedom: Gen Z has mastered something we're still learningùhow to live without needing external validation. Their comfort with non-traditional career paths, their willingness to prioritize mental health over professional advancement, their ability to create meaning independent of institutional approvalùthese aren't character flaws. They're advanced life skills.

Productive skepticism: While our skepticism sometimes leads to cynicism or withdrawal, Gen Z has learned to channel skepticism into improvement. They question systems not to destroy them, but to make them work better. Their approach to social issues, workplace culture, and political engagement reflects this productive questioning.

Comfort with uncertainty: We often need plans, predictions, and guarantees to feel secure. Gen Z has learned to thrive without them. Their ability to adapt quickly, to hold multiple possibilities simultaneously, to engage fully with projects that might not succeedùthese are precisely the skills our uncertain world requires.

Inclusive thinking: Growing up in diverse, connected communities taught them collaboration across differences as a basic life skill. Their natural ability to work with people unlike themselves, to consider multiple perspectives simultaneously, to build consensus without requiring conformityùthese represent advanced social intelligence.

Building Bridges

The path forward isn't choosing sides in generational warfare. It's combining generational strengths instead of defending generational differences.

For Boomers: Share your institutional memory and hard-won wisdom about building lasting systems, but remain open to learning their flexibility and comfort with change. Your experience with creating stability is valuable; their experience with navigating instability is equally valuable.

For Gen X: Share your resourcefulness and self-reliance skills, but learn from their authenticity and emotional intelligence. Your ability to function independently is crucial; their ability to function authentically is equally crucial.

For Millennials: Share your technological optimism and collaborative skills, but learn from their realistic assessment of limitations and contradictions. Your belief in positive change is essential; their clear-eyed view of obstacles is equally essential.

Universal principle: Replace judgment with curiosity. When you find yourself thinking ôthey just don't understand,ö ask instead ôwhat are they understanding that I'm missing?ö

The Path Forward Together

We created the world that required Gen Z's response. The economic decisions, social changes, family structures, and institutional transformations we lived throughùthese weren't mistakes. They were responses to historical circumstances that demanded adaptation.

But recognizing our role isn't about accepting blameùit's about understanding cause and effect. Every major decision we made as a society, from ending the gold standard to championing women's rights to creating dual-income households, had consequences that rippled through generations. Gen Z nihilism is one of those consequences.

The opportunity in front of us is unprecedented: combining generational strengths instead of defending generational differences. We have institutional memory, they have adaptability. We have systems-building experience, they have systems-questioning skills. We have hard-won optimism about human potential, they have clear-eyed realism about human limitations.

Maybe we needed to create philosophers, and we succeeded beyond our wildest dreams. Gen Z's nihilismùtheir refusal to accept easy answers, their comfort with contradiction, their insistence on authentic meaningùmight be exactly what we need to navigate the uncertain future we're all heading into together.

The question isn't whether we can fix Gen Z's nihilism. The question is whether we're wise enough to learn from it.
